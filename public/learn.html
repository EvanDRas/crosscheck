1 · How to read a verdict

The 0–100 score is a weighted sum of six categories — Valuation 20, Profitability 20, Growth 20, Financial health 15, Momentum 10, Analyst view 15. Each metric is mapped to 0–100 through a fixed curve, and the detail line under every bar shows each input and the score it mapped to. If a category has no data, its weight is redistributed rather than scored as zero.

Verdict bands are calibrated so each label is a percentile of the universe: STRONG BUY is the top tier of scores, STRONG SELL the bottom. A verdict is therefore a ranking of the present, not a forecast — a STRONG BUY means the numbers look better than most companies' numbers today.

2 · Price versus value: P/E, P/S, PEG

A share price alone is meaningless — a $900 stock can be cheap and a $9 stock expensive. Valuation ratios divide price by something the business produces. P/E divides by profit: at 30× you pay $30 per $1 of annual earnings. P/S divides by revenue, useful when there is no profit yet. PEG divides P/E by growth, asking whether a high price is earned by high growth.

The trap: cheap is often cheap for a reason. Intel has spent years looking inexpensive while its business shrank; Nvidia has looked expensive for a decade while compounding. The formula uses all three ratios together, but the honest answer is that valuation alone has never been enough to predict returns.

3 · Is the business any good? Margins, ROE, growth

Net margin is the share of each sales dollar kept as profit. Return on equity is profit earned on the owners' capital. Revenue growth is the hardest number to fake, because sales are harder to massage than earnings. Together they describe a machine: how much it makes, how efficiently, and whether it is getting bigger.

Compare within an industry or not at all. Visa runs net margins above 50%; Walmart runs near 2%; both are superb businesses. The Compare button on every result page (it needs a free Finnhub key) puts a company next to its actual peers, which is the only comparison that means anything.

4 · Can it survive a bad year? Current ratio, debt

The current ratio (short-term assets ÷ short-term liabilities) asks whether the company can pay next year's bills from what it already holds. Debt-to-equity asks how much of the company is funded by lenders rather than owners. Debt is cheap fuel in good times and a wrecking ball in bad ones. The front page's The economy right now card shows the current price of that fuel — the Fed's rate — straight from FRED.

Boeing is the textbook case: a great franchise whose balance sheet was hollowed out by buybacks and then crushed by the 737 MAX grounding and the pandemic. Health metrics never predicted the crash — but they showed there was no cushion when it came.

5 · Momentum and the 52-week range — tested, found wanting

Where a price sits in its one-year range is the formula's only momentum input. The folk wisdom says winners keep winning. Crosscheck tested that claim on 274 months of S&P data: the edge was statistically zero (t = −0.24). Momentum's small 10% weight was set before that test — and the test is why you should read the momentum bar with the most skepticism of the six.

The lesson is bigger than momentum: every popular "signal" deserves this treatment. If a rule sounds obvious and profitable, ask who tested it and whether they used only information available at the time.

6 · What an analyst "Buy" actually means

Wall Street ratings skew optimistic by construction: Sells are rare because firms want access to management and investment-banking business. A stock with 30 Buys and 2 Holds is normal, not remarkable. What carries information is the drift — ratings moving toward Sell over months — which the analyst card shows as a recent-months trend.

7 · Reading SEC filings: 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, Form 4

Public companies must tell the SEC the truth, in writing, on a schedule. The 10-K is the annual report (audited, comprehensive). The 10-Q is the quarterly update. An 8-K is a "something happened" notice — acquisitions, CEO departures, restatements — filed within days. A Form 4 is an insider's own trade, filed within two business days.

Crosscheck pulls fundamentals directly from these filings and cross-checks them against the data vendor. When the two disagree, the result page says so (the ⚠︎ marks). That disagreement is itself a lesson: "official" numbers come in several versions. On the front page, the news desk's Filings tab streams what your followed companies filed — an 8-K often lands before the story written about it — and the Insider radar card watches Form 4s for clustered buying.

8 · Look-ahead bias: the mistake that makes fake edges

A company's third-quarter results are not known on September 30. They are known when the 10-Q is filed, often six weeks later. A backtest that uses September 30 data on September 30 is quietly using the future — and will "discover" strategies that could never have been traded.

The Time Machine is built to make this concrete: pick any stock and any date since 2010 — it is the one feature that needs the free Tiingo key from the setup card — and it reconstructs the verdict from filings filed by that day and prices through that close. Then it shows what happened next. Try NVDA on 2023-01-03: the formula said SELL — the stock then beat the S&P by roughly +1,370% (Study 2). That is not a bug in the demo — it is the point.

9 · Total return and the only question that matters

A stock that rises 5% while the index rises 8% lost — an index fund would have done better with no effort. So the bar is never "did it go up" but "did it beat the alternative." And the alternative's return includes dividends, which price charts ignore. That is total return, and the difference between yours and the benchmark's is excess return.

Every call on the track record page is graded this way, split-adjusted, over the same window as the S&P. Losses are published next to wins. If you keep your own calls there too, time grades you the same way. The Your portfolio card on the front page asks the same question about money you actually hold — the same dollars in an S&P fund instead, dividends counted.

10 · What this site found when it tested itself

Crosscheck ran its own formula on 16,497 point-in-time calls from 2011 to 2024. STRONG BUY calls returned −0.92% per quarter versus the market (t = −1.98). Accuracy — did the call's direction beat the index? — was 46% at three months against a 48% base rate. A coin flip, slightly worse at the top confidence level.

Most tools never run this test, or run it and bury the result. The finding is the most useful thing on this site: a transparent, well-built summary of today's fundamentals still does not predict next quarter's price. And every backtest figure here is gross of trading costs and taxes — real results would be worse. Markets are hard in exactly this way. Use the score to understand a company, and use the evidence page to stay humble about what any score can do.

11 · How to use this site without fooling yourself

  1. Read the score as a description of today, never as a forecast.
  2. Click Compare before judging any ratio — high or low only means something against peers.
  3. Check the Trajectory section: one year of growth is a data point, five is a trend.
  4. Look for ⚠︎ marks — when two sources disagree, find out why before trusting either.
  5. Insider buying clusters matter; insider selling almost never does.
  6. Remember what the benchmark means: everything here is graded against an S&P 500 index fund because that is the no-effort alternative. Beating it is the bar; matching it is the sane default.
  7. Log your own calls on the track record page and let time grade them. It is the fastest way to learn whether your judgment beats an index fund — most people's does not, and finding out early is cheap.
